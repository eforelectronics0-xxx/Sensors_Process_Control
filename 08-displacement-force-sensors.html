1Displacement Sensors

Definition A displacement sensor measures the change of position (linear or angular movement) of an object from a reference point and converts it into an electrical signal.

Displacement sensors are classified as resistive (potentiometer), inductive (LVDT, eddy current), capacitive, optical (encoder, laser) and ultrasonic.

1.1 LVDT — Linear Variable Differential Transformer

Construction: It consists of one primary winding in the centre and two identical secondary windings (S1 and S2) wound on a hollow cylindrical former. A soft iron core is placed inside the former and is free to move along the axis; the core is attached to the object whose displacement is to be measured. The two secondaries are connected in series opposition (differential).

Working: An AC supply (1 to 10 kHz, 3 to 15 V) is given to the primary. When the core is exactly in the middle (null position) the voltages induced in S1 and S2 are equal and opposite, so the net output is zero. When the core moves to one side, the coupling with that secondary increases, so the differential output voltage rises and its phase tells the direction of movement. The output is linear over a range of ±10 mm to ±250 mm.

1.2 Capacitive displacement sensor

Two parallel plates form a capacitor. If the distance between them (or the overlapping area) changes due to the movement of one plate, the capacitance changes as C = εA/d. This change is converted to voltage by an oscillator or a bridge circuit. Very high resolution (nanometre) but very sensitive to dust and humidity.

1.3 Optical encoder

A disc with alternate transparent and opaque slots rotates with the shaft. An LED and a photodiode on either side produce pulses as the slots pass. Counting the pulses gives the angular displacement and the pulse rate gives the speed. Two channels 90° apart give the direction.

2Force Sensors

Definition A force sensor measures a push or pull force and gives an output proportional to it. In practice the force is first converted into a deflection of an elastic element and the deflection is then measured by a strain gauge, LVDT or piezoelectric crystal.

The elastic element is called the force summing device or proof body. Common shapes are the proving ring, the cantilever beam, the column and the diaphragm. The complete assembly of proof body plus strain gauges plus bridge circuit is the load cell studied in Topic 3.

Applications

  • Weighing scales, weigh bridges, hopper and tank weighing.
  • Universal testing machines, press force monitoring, torque wrenches.
  • Robot gripper force control, tactile sensors.
  • Touch screens and force feedback joysticks (piezoelectric).
Difference to remember Displacement sensor tells "how much it moved", force sensor tells "how hard it was pushed". Both often use the same elastic element — a load cell measures the displacement of a beam caused by the force.

3Worked numericals

Problem 1. An LVDT with sensitivity 40 mV/mm/V has an excitation of 5 V. The core moves 3 mm from null. Find the output voltage.

Solution. Sensitivity referred to 5 V = 40 × 5 = 200 mV/mm. Vout = 200 mV/mm × 3 mm = 600 mV (phase indicates direction). Vout = 600 mV (0.6 V, sign/phase gives direction).

Problem 2. A capacitive gap sensor has C = 100 pF at nominal gap d = 1 mm with area A fixed. Find C when the gap changes to 1.05 mm, and the fractional change.

Solution. C ∝ 1/d, so C' = C × d/d' = 100 × 1/1.05 = 95.2 pF. ΔC/C = −Δd/d = −0.05/1 = −0.05 = −5 %. Half the fractional gap change appears as output in a simple differential arrangement. C' = 95.2 pF, ΔC/C = −5 %.

4Viva questions and answers

Q1. How does an LVDT work?
A. A movable core changes the mutual inductance of two secondary coils; the difference of the two secondary voltages is a signed measure of core position.

Q2. Why is there no wear in an LVDT?
A. The core never touches the coils — coupling is purely magnetic, so life is practically unlimited.

Q3. What is the principle of a capacitive displacement sensor?
A. Capacitance varies inversely with electrode gap; a bridge or C-to-V converter detects sub-nanometre changes.

Q4. How does an incremental encoder differ from an absolute encoder?
A. Incremental counts steps from power-on and loses position at power-off; absolute gives a unique code for every shaft angle at any time.

Q5. Where is torque measured on a rotating shaft?
A. With strain-gauge torque flanges that transmit data wirelessly, avoiding slip rings and their maintenance.

Q6. Give two industrial uses of force sensors.
A. Weighing platforms and material-testing machines (tensile/compression rigs).

5Summary

This topic covered the measurement of how far something moves, how hard it is pushed and how strongly it is twisted. LVDTs, capacitive probes and encoders cover position and displacement with different resolutions and contact arrangements, while force and torque almost always rest on strain-gauge bridges in elastic bodies. All of them must be mounted stiffly enough that the sensor actually sees the motion or load it is supposed to measure.
