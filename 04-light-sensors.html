Topic 4 · Light SensorsLDR and Photodiode

1Introduction

A light sensor converts the intensity of light falling on it into an electrical quantity (resistance, current or voltage). The three most used devices are the LDR (photoresistor), the photodiode and the phototransistor. Solar cells and opto-couplers also belong to this family.

2LDR — Light Dependent Resistor

2.1 Construction

An LDR is made of a thin layer of high resistance semiconductor such as cadmium sulphide (CdS) or cadmium selenide deposited on a ceramic insulating substrate. On this layer a metallic contact is deposited in a zig-zag (comb like) pattern so that a long conducting path is obtained in a small area. The whole assembly is sealed inside a transparent glass or plastic envelope. Two leads come out of it, so the LDR is a two terminal, non-polar device — it can be connected in any direction.

2.2 Working

The working of an LDR is based on the photo-conductive effect. When photons of light having energy greater than the band gap of the semiconductor strike the layer, electron-hole pairs are created. These extra charge carriers increase the conductivity, so the resistance falls as the light intensity increases. In complete darkness the resistance is called the dark resistance (about 1 MΩ to 10 MΩ) and in bright light it falls to a few hundred ohms.

Because the LDR is a passive device, it is used in a potential divider circuit to obtain a varying voltage.

2.3 Applications

  • Automatic street lights — lamp ON at night, OFF in day.
  • Camera exposure (light meter) control.
  • Burglar alarm using a light beam which is interrupted.
  • Smoke detectors (light scattering type), flame detectors.
  • Contrast and brightness control in photocopiers.

3Photodiode and Phototransistor (brief)

A photodiode is a PN junction diode operated in reverse bias. Light creates electron-hole pairs which are swept across the junction and produce a small reverse current proportional to light intensity. It is very fast (nano-seconds) and is used in optical communication, barcode readers and encoders. A phototransistor is the same idea but with transistor amplification built in, so it gives a much larger current (100 times) but is slightly slower.

4Worked numericals

Problem 1. An LDR has 1 kΩ in bright light and 200 kΩ in darkness. It is used with a 10 kΩ series resistor across a 5 V supply. Find Vout in both cases (voltage across the LDR).

Solution. Bright: Vout = 5 × 1 / (1 + 10) = 0.45 V. Dark: Vout = 5 × 200 / (200 + 10) = 4.76 V. A comparator with threshold near 2.5 V therefore gives a clean light/dark digital output. Vbright = 0.45 V, Vdark = 4.76 V.

Problem 2. A photodiode with responsivity R = 0.55 A/W at 850 nm is illuminated with 200 µW of laser power. Find the photocurrent and the voltage across a 10 kΩ load resistor.

Solution. Iph = R × P = 0.55 × 200×10−6 = 110 µA. Vout = Iph × RL = 110×10−6 × 10×103 = 1.1 V Response time of a reverse-biased diode is in nanoseconds, much faster than an LDR. Iph = 110 µA, Vout = 1.1 V.

5Viva questions and answers

Q1. Why is a photodiode operated in reverse bias?
A. Reverse bias widens the depletion layer, reduces junction capacitance and makes the response much faster; photocurrent is then almost linear with light power.

Q2. What is the dark current?
A. The small current that flows with no light, caused by thermal generation; it sets the lowest detectable light level.

Q3. Why does an LDR response depend on light history?
A. CdS has a memory effect — resistance rises and falls slowly, so it is slow to recover after bright light, which makes it unsuitable for fast signals.

Q4. How does a phototransistor differ from a photodiode?
A. Base light current is amplified by the transistor action, giving 100× more sensitivity but slower speed and less linearity.

Q5. Name an application where a photodiode array is used.
A. Position-sensing, barcode readers, optical encoders and short-range fibre-optic links.

Q6. What is the photovoltaic mode?
A. The diode is left unbiased and generates a voltage like a small solar cell; it is very linear but slower than reverse-biased operation.

6Summary

Light measurement uses either a photoconductor whose resistance falls with illumination (LDR) or junction devices that generate a photocurrent (photodiode, phototransistor). Linearity, speed and spectral response decide which family fits the job: LDRs for simple switching, photodiodes for fast and proportional measurement. Dark current, load resistance and bias conditions together set the smallest light level the circuit can reliably see.
