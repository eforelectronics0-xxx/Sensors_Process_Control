Topic 1 · Introduction to SensorsNeed, Definition, Types of Sensors

1Introduction — Why do we need sensors?

Every automatic system in the world, from a small water level controller in our house to a huge chemical plant, works on one simple idea: first measure, then decide, then act. The part which does the "measuring" job is the sensor. Without a sensor the controller is like a person with closed eyes — it cannot know what is happening in the process, so it cannot take a correct decision.

The need of sensors can be written in the following simple points:

  • To measure a physical quantity (temperature, pressure, level, flow, position, force etc.) which the human eye cannot read continuously.
  • To convert that quantity into an electrical signal which wires, controllers and computers can understand.
  • To give safety — for example a gas sensor alarms before a leakage becomes dangerous, an over-temperature sensor cuts off a heater before the vessel bursts.
  • To give quality control — a process runs correctly only when its temperature, pH, pressure and flow stay inside limits.
  • To give feedback to the controller, so that automatic (closed loop) control becomes possible.
  • To record data for study, billing and troubleshooting.

2Definition of Sensor, Transducer and Transmitter

Definition — Sensor A sensor is a device that senses or detects a physical quantity or a chemical condition of the environment and gives an output signal (mostly electrical) which is a function of that quantity. In simple words, a sensor is the sense organ of a machine.
Definition — Transducer A transducer is a device which converts one form of energy into another form of energy. Example: a microphone converts sound energy into electrical energy.
Note (very often asked in exam) Every sensor is a transducer, but every transducer is not a sensor. A loudspeaker is a transducer (electrical → sound) but it is not a sensor. A sensor always takes information from the process. A transmitter is a transducer plus signal conditioning circuit which gives a standard output such as 4–20 mA or 0–10 V so that the signal can travel far without noise.

Basic building blocks of a sensor system

Any measuring system can be drawn as three blocks in a line: the sensing element which faces the process, the conversion element which makes the electrical signal, and the manipulation / signal conditioning element which cleans and scales the signal before it goes to the display or controller.

4How a sensor is studied (method for every topic)

For every sensor in this unit we shall follow the same four headings, which is also the pattern followed in the university question paper:

  1. Diagram — the physical construction and the electrical circuit.
  2. Construction — which materials and parts are used.
  3. Working — the principle on which it converts the quantity into a signal.
  4. Applications — where it is actually used in industry and daily life.
Remember A sensor must be (i) sensitive only to the quantity to be measured, (ii) fast enough, (iii) rugged enough to survive the plant atmosphere, and (iv) cheap enough to be used in large numbers. No sensor is perfect; selecting a sensor is always a compromise between accuracy, speed, cost and life.

5Worked numericals

Problem 1. An ammeter has a range of 0–10 A and is marked accuracy class 0.5. Find the limiting error when it reads 6 A.

Solution. Maximum permissible error = 0.5 % of full scale = 0.5 × 10 / 100 = 0.05 A. So the true value lies between 6.00 − 0.05 = 5.95 A and 6.00 + 0.05 = 6.05 A. Percentage error at this reading = 0.05 / 6.00 × 100 = 0.83 %. Error = ±0.05 A (0.83 % at the 6 A reading).

6Viva questions and answers

Q1. What is the difference between a sensor and a transducer?
A. A sensor directly senses the physical quantity; a transducer converts one form of energy into another. Many books use the two words loosely, but every transducer that also senses is treated as a sensor.

Q2. Why is a transmitter used ahead of a controller?
A. To convert the weak sensor signal into a standard, noise-resistant signal such as 4–20 mA or 1–5 V so it can travel long cable lengths without loss.

7Summary

In this topic we built the foundation for the whole subject: a sensor is the measuring eye of an automatic system, the measurement chain adds error block by block, and instruments are specified by range, span, accuracy class and uncertainty. Choosing a sensor is a four-question exercise — quantity, range, tolerance and output form — and every later topic on temperature, pressure, flow or control simply applies this same framework to one physical quantity at a time.
