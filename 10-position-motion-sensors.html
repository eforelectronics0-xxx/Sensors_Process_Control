1Position Sensors

Definition A position sensor detects the location of an object with respect to a reference point and converts it into an electrical signal. Position may be linear (along a straight line) or rotary / angular (about an axis).

A position sensor is basically a displacement sensor used inside a feedback loop of a control system. The most common ones are:

1.1 Potentiometric position sensor

A resistive track with a sliding wiper is used. The wiper is coupled to the moving object. The output voltage is Vout = Vin × (x / L), so the voltage is directly proportional to position. It is cheap and gives a large output, but the wiper wears out and the track gets noisy.

1.2 Hall effect position sensor

When a current carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, a voltage appears across it at right angles to both the current and the field: VH = (B I) / (n e t). A small Hall IC (A3144, SS49E) is kept near a rotating magnet. Whenever the magnet passes, the IC gives a pulse. Counting the pulses gives the position and speed — this is how the BLDC motor and the bicycle speedometer work.

2Motion (Speed) Sensors

Motion means the change of position with time, so a motion sensor measures speed (rpm or m/s) and sometimes direction. Important types:

  • Tachogenerator (DC tachometer) — a small DC generator coupled to the shaft; the generated EMF is proportional to speed. Simple and rugged.
  • Optical / magnetic encoder — pulses counted per second give speed; two channels 90° apart give direction.
  • Variable reluctance (VR) sensor — a coil with a magnet placed near a toothed wheel; each passing tooth induces a voltage pulse. Used as the crankshaft position sensor in cars.
  • Hall effect sensor — digital, very reliable, used in BLDC motors.
  • Accelerometer / gyro (IMU) — measures linear acceleration and angular velocity, used in drones and mobile phones.
Speed (rpm) = (pulses per second × 60) / (number of slots)

Applications

  • CNC machines, robots, 3D printers — position feedback of axes.
  • Motor speed control in washing machines, fans, electric vehicles.
  • Crankshaft and camshaft position sensing in petrol and diesel engines.
  • Proximity switches on conveyor lines to count bottles or packets.
  • Parking sensors and drone altitude hold (ultrasonic).

3Worked numericals

Problem 1. A 10 kΩ linear potentiometer of travel 100 mm is fed with 5 V. The wiper is at 35 mm from the zero end. Find Vout.

Solution. Vout = Vin × x/L = 5 × 35/100 Vout = 1.75 V Sensitivity = 5 V / 100 mm = 50 mV per mm. Vout = 1.75 V.

Problem 2. A 1024-pulse-per-revolution incremental encoder is read for 0.2 s and counts 1200 pulses. Find shaft speed in rpm.

Solution. Revolutions = 1200 / 1024 = 1.1719 rev in 0.2 s. Speed = 1.1719 / 0.2 = 5.859 rev/s. rpm = 5.859 × 60 = 351.6 rpm. N ≈ 352 rpm.

Problem 3. A Hall sensor closes at Bop = 35 mT with a magnet that decays 1.5 % per year. Estimate how many years before a 2 mm gap increase causes a miss, given field ∝ 1/r³ from an initial gap of 4 mm.

Solution. Field at 4 mm → 6 mm: ratio = (4/6)³ = 0.296 — far below any margin. In practice a 25 % field margin is designed in, tolerating roughly 8 % gap growth. Gap grows mainly from vibration loosening, not from magnet ageing; check torque on brackets during PM. Mechanical gap control matters more than magnet ageing — verify 4 mm gap in PM.

4Viva questions and answers

Q1. Difference between absolute and incremental encoding?
A. Absolute encoders report the true angle at every instant, even after power loss; incremental encoders only count changes and need a reference at start-up.

Q2. What are quadrature signals?
A. Two pulse channels 90° apart; their phase order reveals direction and combining edges gives 2× or 4× resolution.

Q3. How does a tachogenerator measure speed?
A. It is a small generator whose output voltage is proportional to shaft speed, giving an immediate analog rpm signal.

Q4. Why use a Hall sensor for speed pickup?
A. Non-contact, no wear, works through dust and oil where optical slotted wheels would clog.

Q5. What is homing in motion control?
A. Moving an axis to a fixed reference switch at start-up to establish the coordinate origin before absolute work begins.

Q6. Where are resolvers preferred over encoders?
A. In hot, vibrating environments such as traction motors — they are rugged rotating transformers with no optical parts.

5Summary

Position sensing answers where and speed sensing answers how fast. Potentiometers give simple absolute voltage, Hall devices give contactless digital edges, and encoders give high-resolution quadrature counts for servo loops. Correct gaps, screened cables and a reliable homing procedure are what keep the numbers the controller sees equal to the real shaft or table position.
