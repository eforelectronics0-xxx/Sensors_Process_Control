Topic 5 · Chemical SensorsPrinciple, Types, Working and Applications

1Definition and Need

Definition A chemical sensor is a device that converts chemical information (concentration of a specific chemical component or species in a sample) into an analytical signal which is usually electrical. The chemical information may be composition, concentration, presence of a particular ion or gas.

Chemical sensors are needed wherever we have to know what is inside a substance and not merely its physical condition — for example the pH of water, the oxygen content of blood, the sugar in juice, the alcohol in breath, or the carbon monoxide in a room. Physical sensors cannot answer these questions.

2Construction (general structure)

Almost every chemical sensor has two main parts. The first is the receptor (recognition) element, which selectively reacts with the chemical species to be measured. The second is the transducer element, which converts that chemical reaction into an electrical signal. Between them we often need a selective membrane which allows only the wanted species to pass, so that other substances do not disturb the reading.

4Working of two important examples

4.1 Potentiometric sensor (pH electrode)

A thin glass membrane separates two solutions of different hydrogen ion concentration. A potential difference develops across this membrane which depends on the pH of the test solution. This voltage is measured against a stable reference electrode and is about 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C.

4.2 Amperometric sensor (Clark dissolved oxygen electrode)

Oxygen diffuses through a membrane and is reduced at a platinum cathode. The resulting current is directly proportional to the oxygen concentration. Used in water treatment and blood gas analysis.

5Applications

  • Water quality monitoring — pH, chlorine, dissolved oxygen, turbidity.
  • Medical — blood glucose meter, blood gas analyser, breath analyser.
  • Environmental safety — CO, CO₂, H₂S, NH₃, toxic gas alarms in mines and chemical plants.
  • Food industry — freshness, ripeness, alcohol and sugar content.
  • Automobile — oxygen (lambda) sensor in the exhaust for fuel control.
Two words to remember Selectivity — the sensor must respond only to the wanted species. Drift — the slow change of reading with time, which makes frequent calibration necessary. Both these problems are the biggest drawbacks of chemical sensors.

6Worked numericals

Problem 1. A pH electrode follows E = E0 + 0.05916 × pH at 25 °C. A process sample reads pH 7.00 on the reference and pH 10.00 on the process side. Find the electrode voltage difference.

Solution. ΔpH = 10.00 − 7.00 = 3.00 pH units. ΔE = 0.05916 × 3.00 = 0.1775 V = 177.5 mV. The sign is negative for rising pH on a standard glass electrode (about −59.16 mV per pH unit). |ΔE| = 177.5 mV (−59.16 mV per pH at 25 °C).

Problem 2. A dissolved-oxygen probe gives 8.2 µA at saturation (9.2 mg/L) and 3.1 µA in the sample. Find the oxygen concentration.

Solution. Assume current is linear with concentration: C = Csat × I / Isat C = 9.2 × 3.1 / 8.2 = 3.49 mg/L Temperature compensation corrects saturation value if the sample is not at the calibration temperature. Dissolved oxygen ≈ 3.5 mg/L.

7Viva questions and answers

Q1. What is a reference electrode?
A. A stable electrode of known potential (e.g. Ag/AgCl) that provides a fixed baseline so the sensing electrode voltage can be interpreted absolutely.

Q2. Why does a pH electrode need a high-impedance amplifier?
A. Glass electrodes can source almost no current; any load would collapse the millivolt signal, so input impedance must be 1012 Ω or higher.

Q3. Distinguish potentiometric and amperometric sensors.
A. Potentiometric measures an open-circuit voltage (no current, logarithmic in concentration); amperometric measures a current at fixed bias (linear in concentration).

Q4. What is the Clark electrode used for?
A. Measuring dissolved oxygen in water and blood through a membrane-covered cathode that reduces oxygen and produces a proportional current.

Q5. How do you calibrate a pH sensor?
A. Two-point buffer calibration, usually pH 4.0 and 7.0 (or 7.0 and 10.0), setting slope and offset.

Q6. Name one hazard when chemical sensors foul.
A. Drift and false readings; membranes coated by oil, scale or biological film stop responding and must be cleaned or replaced.

8Summary

Chemical sensors convert concentration or activity into an electrical quantity by one of two routes: a selective membrane that develops a voltage (potentiometric, e.g. pH electrode) or a controlled reaction that draws a current (amperometric, e.g. Clark cell). Both need stable references, careful calibration and clean sensing surfaces. Choosing the right interface circuit — high-impedance amplifier for voltage-type sensors, transimpedance amplifier for current-type sensors — is half the design job.
